What happened to those who studied in a school without classes, without tests, and without grades

Vladimir Dietrich · August 30, 2026 ·7 min read
Português|English

This article is not mine: it is a summary, in Portuguese, of a study by Peter Gray and David Chanoff. The research is theirs — published in the American Journal of Education in 1986, about what became of the graduates of Sudbury Valley School. My work here was to read the original article, summarize the findings, and translate the cited speeches, because there was nothing of this in Portuguese. Everything that follows is the authors' conclusion, except for the last section, where I explain why I went after this.

Sudbury Valley School opened in 1968 in Framingham, Massachusetts. It has no curriculum, compulsory classes, tests, grades, or report cards. Children decide what to do all day, from 4 to 19 years old, and the school is governed by an assembly where each student and each staff member has exactly one vote.

The question every parent asks about this is always the same: what then? Peter Gray, professor of psychology at Boston College, and David Chanoff went after the answer: they sought out, one by one, the graduates from the school's first eleven years. It is the most cited study on the subject, and what follows is what they found.

How the study was conducted

Between June 1970 and June 1981, 82 people left the school as graduates — 78 with diplomas and 4 who left at age 16 or older without presenting a final thesis. The researchers located 76 of them and obtained responses from 69: 91% of those located, 84% of the total. There were 47 written questionnaires, 15 phone interviews, and 15 personal interviews.

The graduates were divided into two groups: those who entered the school up to 6th grade (group 1, those who spent more time there) and those who entered from 7th grade onwards (group 2).

How much time they spent studying “subjects”

Little, and that's not a figure of speech: 29% of group 1 and 56% of group 2 stated they had not taken any courses or tutoring at school. When asked to estimate how much time they dedicated to recognizably academic activities, the average of the responses was 8%.

The results

Just over 50% went to college

They had completed or were attending an undergraduate degree at the time of the research — 64% of group 1 and 47% of group 2. Another 25% had done some higher education study without being enrolled in an undergraduate course.

86% and 54% entered selective colleges

This is the proportion of enrollments from group 1 (6 out of 7) and group 2 (13 out of 24) that occurred in one of the approximately 220 most selective institutions in the United States, according to the 1982 Fiske guide.

And without any academic record with grades

About half first entered a less selective college and then transferred, using their performance there as credentials. The others entered directly, with SAT scores, letters of recommendation, and, in some cases, interviews or tests administered by the college itself. Among those who already had a bachelor's degree, six were in postgraduate studies or already titled — two of them doctoral students, in theoretical physics and anthropology.

None regretted it

To the final question — “overall, are you happy to have studied at SVS instead of a more traditional school?” —, 56 answered “yes, very much”, 11 answered “yes, moderately”, 2 left it blank, and none answered that it would have been better not to have studied there.

And the downsides? The study also records them

This is the part often omitted by those who promote the school, and it is precisely what gives credibility to the rest. Of the 35 graduates from the university subgroup, 13 said the school harmed them in some way: eight reported arriving weak in some academic area — mathematics was the most cited; three felt disadvantaged by not having an academic record when applying; and two said they felt intimidated at first, finding college very different from school. One of them postponed entry for a few years because of this.

The same 13, without exception, also answered that the school benefited them — and most made it clear that the benefit outweighed the harm. In total, 32 out of 35 said the school benefited them for subsequent studies. The two most cited reasons, each by about half of them: greater desire to continue learning, and the habit of being responsible for one's own education.

What they worked as

Among the 69, careers spread across arts (15), health and care professions (13), management and business (8), administrative work and sales (6), academic career (5), applied science and technology (5), skilled trades (9), and various (8).

Half of the graduates from group 1 — those who spent more years at school — pursued careers in the arts: an international company dancer, a landscape photographer who supported himself with studio portraits, a ceramics apprentice, a rock musician, a pianist and arranger trained by a prestigious conservatory.What they themselves said

“I am grateful to the school for the education it gave me about people, life, and coexistence. I am much more tolerant of those who are different from me. There, everyone is an individual. In public school, being different in any aspect is something to avoid at all costs.”

“The discussion of democratic philosophy, especially the debates about individual responsibility, deeply marked me. I continue to strive hard to know what my responsibilities are and to fulfill them. And this, of course, helped in all areas of my life.”

“I am happy to have gone and to have lived with those people, because it transformed me from a rebellious child into a person in control of my own life. I gained confidence to do whatever I want.”

What this study does not prove

The article is honest about its own limits, and those who cite it should be too. The sample is small and from only one school. The data is self-reported. The researchers had ties to the school and acknowledge in the text the risk of this influencing the responses. And, most importantly, the population is not random: 83% of respondents had at least one parent with a university degree. A family that chooses a school like this is already an unusual family.

What the study allows us to state is more modest — and yet remarkable: growing up without a curriculum, without tests, and without grades did not prevent these people from entering university, pursuing varied professions, or being at peace with their own education. The question “what then?” has, at least, a documented answer.

Original and full reading

Gray, P. & Chanoff, D. (1986). Democratic schooling: what happens to young people who have charge of their own education? American Journal of Education, 94(2), 182–213. doi.org/10.1086/443842

The full article, in English, is available from the author himself in PDF, and has a listing on the Alliance for Self-Directed Education.
